Accessibility Analysis to Nearby Open Water Source

Mon 15 Dec 2025 • 16:19 GMT by Spatiality

Direct Normal Irradiance Distribution. Documentation by Spatiality

Fighting peatland wildfires is an uphill battle. During the dry season, water levels drop significantly—largely driven by drainage canals that transform naturally waterlogged peat into dry, highly flammable terrain. This not only fuels relentless fires that burn deep underground and require prolonged downpours to extinguish, but it also places immense strain on firefighters battling the blaze day and night.

While agencies like BRGM have installed hundreds of dip wells (groundwater pumping points) and canal blocks across degraded peatlands, these installations are primarily designed to maintain baseline water tables during dry spells rather than serve as direct firefighting hydrants. Although dip wells can be used to wet the surrounding area when it hasn't rained for a week, coverage is rarely comprehensive. In many remote areas, finding an open water source during a fire emergency remains a major hurdle due to difficult terrain and long distances.

The Model and Its Parameters

To address this, I developed a model to estimate travel times to the nearest available water source whenever a hotspot emerges. Dip wells, reservoirs, canals, and rivers are all factored in as potential firefighting assets. The model operates under several key assumptions and limitations:
  • Mixed Transportation: Combines walking (to reach passable roads) with vehicular travel, and vice versa.
  • Traffic Congestion: Excluded from the current scope.
  • Water Transport: Excludes boats and major river crossings (such as the Kapuas or Mahakam rivers, or similar major waterways in Indonesia).

Core Functionality and Weighting

Parameter Used in Analysis. Documentation by Spatiality

At its core, the model calculates the estimated travel time from any given pixel to the nearest designated water source. To ensure realistic outputs, the analysis incorporates several weighting factors:

  • Road quality and existing infrastructure
  • Land cover types
  • Drainage Density: Areas with denser canal networks dry out faster, increasing susceptibility (excluding canals with active blocking).
  • Land Utilization: Production forests and agricultural conversion zones face higher risks than strictly protected or conservation forests, largely due to varying levels of active management and legal protection.
  • Canal Block Density: Higher concentrations of effective canal blocks help retain dry-season water tables, lowering fire spread risks.
  • Burn History: Previously burned peatlands are often less prone to immediate re-ignition due to changes in physical and chemical properties, though surface vegetation and regenerating shrubs remain at risk.

All parameters were reclassified and weighted to evaluate land properties under peak dry-season conditions. The model was then calibrated against historical hotspot data to validate the influence of each parameter. While tailored for specific regional characteristics, this framework can be adapted and fine-tuned for other landscapes. Ultimately, the model provides a clear picture of high-risk zones during the dry season, helping local communities and authorities stay a step ahead of potential worst-case scenarios.
